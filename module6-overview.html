Screening, Prevention & Taking Action

The previous modules covered what colorectal cancer is, the trends driving early-onset cases, the research on lifestyle risk factors, and time for personal reflection. This final instructional module focuses on what you can actually do — specifically, how screening works, what the current guidance says, and which lifestyle changes have the strongest evidence behind them.

Screening: The Most Effective Tool Available

Most colorectal cancers develop slowly over years from pre-cancerous polyps. Screening can detect and remove polyps before they become cancer, and can identify cancer at an early, highly treatable stage. This is why public health agencies consider screening the single most impactful intervention for colorectal cancer prevention.

Who Should Be Screened and When?

  • Average risk, age 45–75: Both the American Cancer Society and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommend starting regular screening at age 45.[1][2]
  • Higher risk: If you have a family history of colorectal cancer or advanced polyps, personal history of inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's or ulcerative colitis), or an inherited syndrome such as Lynch syndrome or FAP, earlier and more frequent screening may be recommended. Discuss with a healthcare provider.[2]
  • Any age with symptoms: Symptoms such as rectal bleeding, persistent change in bowel habits, or unexplained weight loss should prompt a conversation with a clinician regardless of age — do not wait until the recommended screening age.[3]

Common Screening Options

Colonoscopy

Gold standard. Examines the entire colon; polyps can be removed during the same procedure. Typically every 10 years if results are normal.[2]

Fecal Immunochemical Test (FIT)

Non-invasive stool test done annually at home. Detects blood in stool that may signal polyps or cancer. Positive results require follow-up colonoscopy.[2]

Stool DNA Test (e.g., Cologuard)

Detects DNA changes and blood in stool. Recommended every 1–3 years.[2]

CT Colonography (Virtual Colonoscopy)

Imaging of the colon using CT scan. Typically every 5 years. Does not allow polyp removal during the procedure.[2]

Prevention: What the Evidence Supports

Approximately 50–70% of colorectal cancer cases are estimated to be attributable to modifiable lifestyle factors. While no lifestyle choice can guarantee prevention, the following changes are supported by strong, convergent evidence from multiple major health organizations.

These risk factors describe population-level patterns, not individual certainty. They help guide prevention decisions, but they do not diagnose or predict any one person's outcome.

Increase dietary fiber

Aim for at least 25–30 g/day from whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables. Each 10 g/day increase is associated with approximately 10% lower CRC risk (WCRF, convincing evidence).

Limit red and processed meat

IARC classifies processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen for CRC. Reducing or avoiding processed meats (bacon, sausages, deli meats) is one of the clearest dietary recommendations.

Stay physically active

The most active individuals have approximately 24% lower colon cancer risk vs. the least active (WCRF, convincing evidence). Aim for regular moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity most days of the week.

Maintain a healthy body weight

Excess abdominal adiposity is a well-established risk factor. Weight management reduces risk through multiple pathways including insulin resistance and chronic inflammation.

Limit or avoid alcohol

IARC classifies alcohol as a Group 1 carcinogen for CRC. No level of alcohol consumption is established as entirely safe from a CRC-risk standpoint (WCRF).

Do not smoke

Long-term tobacco use is associated with increased risk of colorectal polyps and cancer, particularly at higher cumulative exposure. Quitting reduces risk over time.

When to Talk to a Doctor

Speak with a licensed healthcare provider if you:

  • Notice blood in your stool or rectal bleeding that is unexplained.
  • Experience a persistent change in bowel habits lasting more than a few days.
  • Have ongoing abdominal pain, cramping, or bloating without a clear cause.
  • Have unexplained fatigue, weakness, or weight loss.
  • Have a family history of colorectal cancer or polyps, or a known inherited syndrome such as Lynch syndrome or FAP.
  • Are approaching age 45 and have not yet discussed screening options with a provider.
